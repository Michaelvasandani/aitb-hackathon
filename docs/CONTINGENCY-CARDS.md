# Contingency Cards

## How to use this deck

Read the index now. Do not read the deck now. When something goes wrong, find the card, read the three moves, and do them in that order — the order is load-bearing, and doing move 3 first usually wastes move 1.
Each card names a **role**, not a person. Fill in who holds each role before the day; the roster is on the last page.
"Breaks downstream" is the list of things that just went stale. Hand that list to whoever maintains the plan. Do not try to recompute it in your head at 9:05 AM.
Numbers marked `[illustrative]` are examples to adjust, not measurements. Unmarked numbers are what actually happened at the San Diego event.
Aaron Eden, who has run hundreds of these: *"No event goes to plan."* This deck assumes he is right.

**Index:** 1 Early arrivals · 2 Headcount wrong · 3 Food short · 4 Teams not formed · 5 Wi-Fi down · 6 Mentors missing or unbriefed · 7 Judges arrive cold · 8 Sponsor changes registration · 9 Schedule slipped · 10 A question nobody can answer

## CARD 1 — There are participants in the room and check-in is not open

**You'll know because:** People are standing in the space or sitting at tables before your published start time, and the check-in table is either not set up or has nobody behind it. In San Diego, doors were said to be locked until 9:00 and everyone was told 9:15; about 30 people were inside at 9:01, none checked in, and no mentors had arrived — they had been told 9:15 too.
**Decides:** Check-in lead. Lead organizer if the check-in lead has not arrived.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Move the check-in table to where the people already are — the doorway, the lobby, the corridor — and check them in where they are standing. Take the list to them; do not make them come to a table.
2. Put one person at the entrance with a printed name list and a marker, checking names by hand. Paper first, laptop later. Then say one sentence out loud to the room: what time things actually start, where the bathrooms are, where the coffee is.
3. Text every mentor and volunteer one line — "people are already here, come now" — with a specific arrival time, not "soon."
**Breaks downstream:** Run of show (the T-45 check-in window is already spent), check-in list (people in the room who are not on it), the first headcount report, badge and table assignment.
**Prevent next time:** Staff the check-in desk from T-45 minutes and give mentors and volunteers an arrival time earlier than the participant time, because participants will arrive before whatever time you published.

## CARD 2 — The number of people in the room does not match the registration list

**You'll know because:** Half an hour in, the check-in count is well under or over the registration number — or, more commonly, nobody can tell you the count at all when you ask. In San Diego 90 people registered, about 65 participants came, and with mentors the room held roughly 70. Food had been ordered for 60. The lead organizer was texting his own team for a headcount an hour into his own event.
**Decides:** Lead organizer.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Name one person out loud as headcount owner, right now, and give them a clock time to report by. They walk the room and count heads — occupied chairs, not the registration export.
2. Have them count twice and report two numbers: participants, and separately mentors, judges, volunteers and staff. Food goes to everyone in the building, not just participants.
3. Write both numbers, with the time they were taken, somewhere the organizers can see them — whiteboard or a sheet at the check-in desk. Every decision for the next two hours uses that number and no other.
**Breaks downstream:** Food order, team and table count, badge and name-tag count, swag and prize count, the break-even budget line, and the judging schedule (which is a function of how many teams demo).
**Prevent next time:** Assign headcount to a named person with a reporting time before the event, and count the room rather than trusting the registration export.

## CARD 3 — There is not enough food for the people in the building

**You'll know because:** Your headcount is above the catered count, or the line is still long while the trays are half empty. In San Diego this was caught an hour in, and the caterer donated 10 extra meals — luck, not process.
**Decides:** Lead organizer makes the call to the caterer. Food or logistics lead executes in the room.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Stop the self-serve line and have the food portioned and served instead. Hold back a tray for the mentors, judges and volunteers who are still working and will eat last.
2. Call the caterer — phone, not email — with three facts ready: how many meals short, how many people total, and the time you need them by. Ask directly whether they can add meals. Caterers who are already on site frequently can.
3. If they cannot, send one named person with a payment method to the nearest volume option, with a hard number and a return time. Then tell the room the truth and give them a time.
**Breaks downstream:** Budget food line and the break-even sponsor count, run of show (the lunch block stretches), every afternoon block after it, and sponsor-thanks copy if a sponsor's name is on the food.
**Prevent next time:** Order against the confirmed room count plus a margin — 15% is a reasonable start `[illustrative]` — and reconfirm the number with the caterer at a fixed time on the morning of.

## CARD 4 — The build clock has started and teams are not formed

**You'll know because:** People are standing around or sitting alone, the project list is on screen and nobody has moved, and the pre-event voting data is incomplete or missing. In San Diego the voting system had broken upstream, only about 40 of 90 registrants had voted, and teams were shuffled live in the room — "two people move over there." That burns the most expensive hour of the weekend.
**Decides:** Lead organizer, or program lead if one is named.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Abandon the voting data. Put one sheet of paper per project on the wall, spread far apart, and tell people to physically walk to the project they want. Give them five minutes `[illustrative]` and say the number out loud.
2. Walk the wall and rebalance in public: say how many each team still needs, move whole people rather than splitting anyone's attention, and hold the cap on team size.
3. Before anyone sits down, make every team name one internal leader out loud, and write that name on the project sheet. A mentor is not a substitute for a team having its own leader.
**Breaks downstream:** Team roster, mentor assignment map, table and room plan, demo order and judging schedule, and the submission list.
**Prevent next time:** Resolve teams before the room fills — run project selection as a pre-event step with a deadline, and bring a printed default assignment for everyone who did not respond.

## CARD 5 — The room cannot get online

**You'll know because:** Three people at three different tables cannot load a page, or the venue network does not appear or rejects the password. This did not happen in San Diego, but Wi-Fi was named there as the one non-negotiable: everything else on this list can be improvised, and this cannot.
**Decides:** Lead organizer, with the venue contact.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Confirm it is the room and not one laptop — test on two devices in two corners. Then phone the named venue contact directly and ask two questions: what is the status of the guest network, and who can reset it today.
2. Turn on hotspots — yours, the volunteers', the mentors' — and allocate them out loud, teams doing live API work first. Write the hotspot names and passwords on the whiteboard so nobody asks twice.
3. Announce a plan and a check-back time from the front of the room, so you have one person debugging the network instead of the whole room. Tell teams to move to work that does not need a connection: design, writing, local setup, planning the demo.
**Breaks downstream:** Everything that assumes connectivity — the model-credit distribution runbook, the submission process, live judging, and every block in the run of show that depends on those.
**Prevent next time:** Test the network in the actual room before the day, ideally under load, and get a named venue contact with a phone number who can fix it on a weekend.

## CARD 6 — Teams are working and mentors are not with them, or are here but do not know what to do

**You'll know because:** Mentors are standing together near the coffee rather than at tables; a team has not spoken to anyone in an hour; or a mentor asks you what they are supposed to be doing. In San Diego mentor training was optional and, in hindsight, should have been mandatory — and on the morning itself the mentors had been given the same late arrival time as everyone else, so none were present when the room filled.
**Decides:** Mentor lead, or lead organizer if none is named.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Pull every mentor into one standing brief, five minutes `[illustrative]`, and cover four things only: which team you have, what time demos start, what "on track" looks like at each checkpoint, and that the job is to stop rabbit holes rather than to write code.
2. Assign mentors to teams by name, out loud, and write the pairing where the room can see it. Spare mentors go to the least experienced teams, not the most interesting projects.
3. Set a floor: every team gets a visit at fixed times `[illustrative]`, and after each round the mentor reports back one word — on track, stuck, or at risk.
**Breaks downstream:** Mentor assignment map, the mentor checkpoints in the run of show, team progress at demo time, and judging scores.
**Prevent next time:** Make the mentor briefing mandatory and hold it before the first participant is in the room, not alongside them.

## CARD 7 — Judges are arriving with no context on the event they are about to score

**You'll know because:** A judge asks on arrival what the teams built, or what the criteria are. This is the normal case, not the failure case: judges typically show up only for the final hours — in San Diego, 4–6 PM on the Sunday of a two-day event — having missed everything that led to it.
**Decides:** Judging lead, or lead organizer.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Sit them down away from the room for ten minutes `[illustrative]` before they see a single demo. Hand each one a single printed page: the rubric with its weights, the number of teams, the length of each demo, and the prompt the teams were given.
2. Walk the criteria out loud and settle the mechanics before demos begin — score independently, no conferring mid-demo, and who breaks a tie.
3. Give them one paragraph on the days they missed: what tools the teams had, what constraints they worked under, and what a reasonable result looks like in that amount of time. Judges who do not know the constraint score the wrong thing.
**Breaks downstream:** How the judging rubric actually gets applied, final scores, the demo schedule (the briefing eats its buffer), the prize announcement, and the sponsor-thanks moment attached to it.
**Prevent next time:** Send judges a one-page or short recorded orientation with the rubric, the timeline and the constraints in advance, and confirm each one opened it.

## CARD 8 — A sponsor has changed where people register or what participant data you may have

**You'll know because:** Registration is moved onto the sponsor's own page, or you are told you cannot export or share the participant list, or the list you receive no longer matches the form you built. In San Diego a sponsorship landed at T-3 weeks; registration moved to the sponsor's site, the data-sharing rules changed, and the project-voting system broke. 90 registered, about 40 voted, headcount became unknown, food was ordered for 60, and roughly 70 people were in the room. One upstream change, five downstream failures.
**Decides:** Lead organizer, with the sponsor contact.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Get in writing exactly what you will receive and when: names, emails, counts, dietary needs, and the time you get them. Until proven otherwise, assume you get a number and nothing else.
2. List every artifact built on top of the old registration — project voting, headcount, badges, dietary counts, the mailing list — and mark each one working or broken. Anything that needed participant data you no longer have is broken, whether or not it looks broken yet.
3. Replace each broken piece with something that runs on paper in the room: a printed sign-in sheet, a project wall for team selection, a hand count for food. Decide these before the day, not on it.
**Breaks downstream:** Registration list, project voting and the team roster, headcount report, food order, badges, dietary requirements, and the participant mailing list that post-event follow-up depends on.
**Prevent next time:** Treat any sponsor landing inside the last month as a change to registration itself, and keep a room-only fallback for team formation and headcount that never depends on a system you do not control.

## CARD 9 — The run of show is more than 30 minutes behind and nothing has been cut

**You'll know because:** You are still running the block before the one the clock says, you are quietly shortening lunch to catch up, or you hear yourself say "we'll make it up later." You will not make it up later. Set your own trigger threshold in advance; 30 minutes `[illustrative]` is a reasonable one for a single-day event.
**Decides:** Lead organizer.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Stop trying to catch up by compressing everything. Find the one fixed point that genuinely cannot move — usually demos and judging, because judges have hard end times — and work backwards from it on paper.
2. Cut one whole block rather than trimming four. Announce the cut from the front of the room in one sentence, so it is a decision rather than something people discover.
3. Rewrite the times on whatever schedule is visible in the room — the whiteboard, the printed sheet at the door — so there is exactly one true version and nobody is working from the old one.
**Breaks downstream:** Run of show, demo schedule, the judging window, food service time, venue load-out time, and any sponsor speaking slot.
**Prevent next time:** Build named buffers between blocks instead of scheduling them back to back, and decide in advance which block you will cut first if you run late.

## CARD 10 — Someone is asking a question nobody in the room can answer, and they need it answered now

**You'll know because:** A volunteer is at the front with a participant and the answer is being guessed at, or three people have given three different answers to the same question in ten minutes.
**Decides:** Lead organizer, or the named owner of that domain if there is one.
**Do these three things, in order:**
1. Do not guess. Say "I will get you an answer by [time]" and hold to the time. A confident wrong answer at 9:05 AM is worse than a five-minute wait, because someone will act on it.
2. Route it to the named owner: venue contact for the building, sponsor contact for credits and rules, judging lead for scoring, food lead for meals, check-in lead for the list. If that thing has no owner, name one now and say the name out loud.
3. Once it is answered, if more than one person could plausibly ask it, say it once to the whole room and write it on the visible board so it stays answered.
**Breaks downstream:** Whatever a wrong answer touched — the submission deadline in the run of show, the food count, the judging criteria as participants understood them. If there was no owner to route to, the contact list itself is incomplete and needs a name added.
**Prevent next time:** Post a one-page contact list of named owners with phone numbers at the check-in desk, and tell every volunteer that "I will find out" is a correct and expected answer.
